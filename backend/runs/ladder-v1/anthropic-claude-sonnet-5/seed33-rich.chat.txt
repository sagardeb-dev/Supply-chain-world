========================== WRAP-UP (episode over) ==========================

AGENT
       The 26-week episode is complete.

       **Summary of how it played out:**

       - **Weeks 0–3:** Steady-state ordering (~20–25 units/wk) via Suez on
       the cheap spot contract while the lane was calm.
       - **Week 3–4:** First Suez/Red Sea alarm — paid for a briefing
       (confirmed genuine risk), air-expedited units to cover the gap, and
       shifted new orders to Cape as a hedge. It turned out to be a short
       false alarm that snapped back to normal by week 5.
       - **Weeks 5–12:** Resumed Suez sourcing on spot when the lane read
       normal, used freight locks twice to ride out rate spikes cheaply, and
       used air-expedite whenever inventory got dangerously thin (several
       sharp demand upswings to 30-38/wk hit during this stretch).
       - **Week 13:** An audit revealed spot had turned "slipping" (confirmed
       by falling realized_fill and rising lead-slip) — signed a contract with
       reliable **qualified** and shifted sourcing there, eating the modest
       premium to protect against a supplier that could go defunct.
       - **Weeks 14–19:** Continued qualified-based ordering; ran quality
       inspections when AQL results turned "marginal"; watched a second Red
       Sea alarm turn into the real, long disruption this time (ships diverted
       around the Cape, surcharges hit).
       - **Weeks 19–23:** Fully pivoted to Cape + qualified once the
       disruption was confirmed genuine and sustained; used repeated air-
       expedites to bridge stockout gaps while sea pipeline caught up; lapsed
       the now-unused/degraded spot contract to stop paying dual-source
       overhead.
       - **Weeks 23–26:** Demand suddenly collapsed (structural decline
       signal, forecast fell to single digits) while the pipeline was very
       full from the disruption-era ordering surge. Held all new orders to
       zero for the final four weeks, letting inventory and the in-transit
       pipeline drain down rather than compounding an overstock — accepting
       some unavoidable demurrage on already-committed shipments caught in
       port congestion at the end.

       **Key decisions that shaped the outcome:**
       1. Riding the cheap spot contract early, but exiting it decisively once
       audit/realized-fill data showed real degradation (avoided riding it
       into a "defunct" failure).
       2. Distinguishing false alarms from the real Suez disruption by
       watching whether suez_count snapped back the following week — avoided
       overreacting to the first alarm, then fully committed to Cape once the
       second one was confirmed by the bulletin and diverted-ship statuses.
       3. Using air-expedite tactically as a cheaper alternative to stockout
       penalties during acute shortages, never as a standing strategy (capped,
       disciplined use).
       4. Recognizing the late-episode demand collapse and stopping new orders
       well ahead of the horizon end to minimize terminal excess-inventory and
       holding costs, even though it meant absorbing demurrage on pipeline
       already committed during the disruption.
